Hallucination Testing: A Practical Framework for QA Teams

LLMs fabricate facts with complete confidence. Here's a structured approach to catching hallucinations before they reach production.

Every QA engineer working with LLMs has seen it: the model responds with absolute confidence, citing specific numbers, dates, or policies — and none of it is real. It's not a bug in the traditional sense. The model isn't crashing or returning errors. It's doing exactly what it was designed to do — predict the most likely next token. Sometimes that prediction is a fabrication.

The question for QA teams isn't whether hallucinations will happen. They will. The question is: how do you build a systematic process to detect them before your users do?

Defining what we're testing for

Not all hallucinations are the same. In our work at EXL, we've found it useful to categorize them into three types:

  • Factual fabrication. The model invents facts, statistics, or quotes that don't exist. "According to a 2024 Gartner report..." when no such report exists.
  • Contextual drift. The model starts with accurate information but gradually drifts, mixing in details from unrelated topics or conversations.
  • Confident uncertainty. The model doesn't know the answer but presents a plausible-sounding guess as fact, with no hedging or qualification.

Each type requires a different detection strategy.

The grounded truth approach

The foundation of our hallucination testing framework is what we call grounded truth comparison. The idea is simple: for every test scenario, we maintain a verified knowledge base — the "ground truth" — and compare the model's outputs against it.

This isn't just string matching. We use semantic similarity scoring to determine whether the model's response aligns with the ground truth in meaning, even if the exact words differ. A response that paraphrases the ground truth accurately is fine. A response that sounds similar but introduces a fabricated detail is a hallucination.

Adversarial prompting for edge cases

Ground truth comparison catches hallucinations on known topics. But what about the cases where the model should say "I don't know"? That's where adversarial prompting comes in.

We design prompts specifically intended to elicit hallucinations:

  • Questions about fictional entities presented as real ("What was the outcome of the 2025 Summit on Quantum Ethics in Geneva?")
  • Requests for specifics the model couldn't possibly have ("What exact percentage of EXL's Q4 revenue came from AI services?")
  • Leading questions with false premises ("Since Dialogflow CX discontinued webhook support, how should we handle integrations?")

A well-behaved model should refuse to answer these confidently. If it provides detailed responses, that's a hallucination we've caught before production.

Building it into your sprint

The biggest mistake I see QA teams make with hallucination testing is treating it as a one-time audit. Hallucination patterns change as models are updated, fine-tuned, or given new context. This needs to be continuous.

In our team, every sprint includes a dedicated hallucination testing cycle. We maintain a growing library of adversarial prompts, update our ground truth databases, and track hallucination rates over time. When the rate spikes, we investigate — is it a model update? A change in the prompt template? New training data?

The goal isn't zero hallucinations. That's not realistic with current LLMs. The goal is zero undetected hallucinations in production-critical flows.
